SpaceX targets orbital milestone with Starship flight 14
SpaceX is preparing Starship for its fourteenth test flight with the primary objective of reaching orbital space for the first time in the vehicles more than three years of test history. Standing 407 feet tall when fully stacked, Starship is the largest and most powerful rocket ever built, and an orbital success would mark a critical milestone for NASAs Artemis programme and SpaceXs ambition to transition Florida Starlink launches from Falcon 9 to Starship.
◆ PRISM ANALYSIS
An orbital success for Starship would materially de-risk NASAs Artemis lunar architecture and accelerate the timeline for SpaceX to retire Falcon 9 from Florida Starlink missions, reshaping launch capacity and pricing across the commercial market.

NORMAL Global HIGH · 50
Moon crater found, 222 metres wide, biggest in recent times
NASAs Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter identified a 222-metre-wide, up to 43-metre-deep crater on the lunar near side in May 2024, formed by an asteroid or comet fragment impact that went undetected in real time and was not confirmed until early 2026. Researchers estimate an impact of this scale occurs only once every 132 years, and the crater has been named for the late Thomas McGetchin, former director of the Lunar and Planetary Institute in Houston.
◆ PRISM ANALYSIS
The discovery of a crater of this magnitude going undetected for months underscores the gap in near-real-time lunar monitoring, directly relevant to risk planning for any permanent crewed base that NASA intends to build on the Moon.

◈ WATCH: Starship Flight 14 orbital attempt, NET Sept 22
Starship Flight 14, targeting no earlier than September 22, is the single most consequential near-term test of whether SpaceX can deliver the orbital capability NASA requires for Artemis and whether Starlink-on-Starship transitions from announcement to execution.
▲ BULL CASE  /  ▼ BEAR CASE ▾
▲ BULL CASE
A successful orbital insertion validates Starship as a mature launch platform, accelerates Starlink Florida migration away from Falcon 9, and strengthens the case for Artemis-linked programme timelines, lifting SpaceX-adjacent suppliers and launch-services competitors who must now price orbital reusability into their own roadmaps.
▼ BEAR CASE
Another sub-orbital failure or scrub extends the timeline for Starlink capacity growth, delays NASA Artemis dependencies, and signals to investors in competing heavy-lift programmes that the reusable orbital market remains further out than priced, pressuring valuations across the sector.
